Part one · One drowning, no suspect, a month of coverage

On the Fourth of July, Nolan Wells, 18 and black, boated to Horn Island, Mississippi with white friends, stayed behind when they left, and was found dead in the water two days later. Authorities call it a likely accidental drowning with no foul play suspected; there is no suspect, because investigators do not believe there was a crime. The country treated it as a referendum on American racism anyway: a month of national coverage, a celebrity chorus, Ben Crump, an independent autopsy. By early August, the only criminal charges connected to the case were against three people accused of threatening witnesses, officials, and a judge.

Method. Window: July 1 through July 31, 2026, the calendar month in which Nolan Wells was found dead; his July 6 discovery is marked on the timeline. Cases are placed by incident date; arrests and charges may postdate the window. Every row is verified against court records or official police releases plus at least two independent outlets. Race is coded from booking photos, official booking records, and published photographs, never assumed from names or third-party lists; every photo tile on a case card links to the source bearing that person's photograph. Case discovery drew on news sweeps, official releases, victim memorials, and publicly circulated case lists; no case entered this ledger on any list's say-so, and entries that failed verification were discarded. Every photograph and record behind these codings was reviewed by human eyes; nothing here was coded by algorithm or assumption. Circumstance comes from court records. A case labeled a hate crime by anyone other than a prosecutor is not coded as one here. An independent full-month sweep of mainstream and official sources on August 6 surfaced no additional verifiable cases beyond those counted.

Exclusions. The counting principle is deliberate violence directed at a person: shootings, stabbings, beatings, drownings. A death from a round fired at people counts even if it struck someone unintended; the law calls that transferred intent. A death caused by a vehicle driven drunk or in flight from police does not count in either direction, whatever the charge, because no violence was aimed at anyone; a vehicle used deliberately as a weapon against a person would. Homicides without a publicly identified suspect are not counted. Cases where race cannot yet be confirmed are held. Both directions run through the identical pipeline. The underlying dataset also tracks other-pairing victims, including a third victim of the St. Paul shooting, plus held, unsolved, and vehicular cases; those rows are recorded but not displayed in this view.

Presumption of innocence. Every accused person named here is charged, not convicted, unless a disposition is noted. Charges are allegations.

Objection 3 “Over Half of Homicide Exonerations Are Black” ▼

No. Of the exonerations, half are Black. That does not mean half of the homicides Black Americans commit end up exonerated — it means that the exonerations themselves (which total only about 35 per year nationwide) are split roughly down the middle, with about 19 Black exonerations per year and about 11 White, per the National Registry of Exonerations.

Which makes sense. According to FBI data, Black Americans are the identified offender in roughly 55% of known-race homicides nationally. So 55% of murder exonerations being Black matches the offending base almost exactly — not a sign of systemic wrongful conviction, but a sign that exonerations scale with convictions, which scale with offending.

The statistic is about the tiny population of overturned convictions, not about the much larger population of committed homicides. The reason the exonerations are about half Black is the same reason the convictions are about half Black: Black Americans are the identified offender in roughly half of known-race homicides nationally.

It isn’t that half of Black people are exonerated. It’s that half of the exonerations are of Black defendants — which turns out to be a tiny fraction (0.30%) of total Black homicide offenders. The equivalent White rate is 0.23%. The gap between them is 0.07 percentage points.

Stress-Test Against California

Suppose every single one of the ~19 Black wrongful convictions per year in the entire United States had actually happened in California. Subtract 19 from the Black homicide arrest count every year, then recompute the disproportionality multiplier. The rate doesn’t move.

California Black Homicide Disproportionality — Original vs. Exoneration-Adjusted
For each year 2020–2024: arrest share ÷ 6.0% population share. “Adjusted” subtracts 19 wrongful convictions per year (the entire U.S. annual total) from the California Black count and from the California total.
Even under an impossibly generous assumption — all U.S. Black wrongful convictions happening in California — the disproportionality multiplier stays above 4.4× every year. Removing wrongful convictions slightly increases the rate, because the total homicide count shrinks too.

Objection 6 “This Is the Legacy of American Slavery” ▼
California Never Had Slavery
California entered the Union as a free state in 1850. No plantations. No state-level Black Codes. No Jim Crow statutes. Yet California’s 25-year homicide arrest data shows the same 4.4× disparity as jurisdictions with centuries of slavery behind them. The disparity does not track with slavery history.

Chinese Exclusion · The Counter-Test

In California specifically, Chinese Americans faced legal restrictions more severe than Black Americans on every major dimension of civil rights. If historical discrimination drives present-day offending, this is where the strongest effect should appear.
